## 3. Fact check (for pinned comments or replies)
- 2% (Whillans et al. 2017, study 9): 98 working adults asked how they'd spend a $40 windfall; "Only 2.0% spontaneously reported that they would make a time-saving purchase."
- Survey (studies 1-7): n = 6,271 across the US, Canada, Denmark and the Netherlands. People who paid to outsource disliked daily tasks reported greater life satisfaction (d = 0.24 across studies 1-6), not moderated by income or wealth. Correlational.
- Buffer: time stress went with lower life satisfaction for non-buyers (B = -0.18) but only weakly for buyers (B = -0.03, not significant).
- Millionaires (study 6, n = 818): the text says "just under half" spent nothing outsourcing; Table 1 lists 60.3% as buying time. The video says "a big share".
- Experiment (study 8): 60 working adults in Vancouver, $40 on two consecutive weekends; positive mood 4.00 vs 3.71, time pressure 3.49 vs 4.22 after the time-saving vs the material purchase; the mood gain ran through lower time stress.
- Policymakers (Whillans & West 2022 pilot): 30 current and aspiring policymakers at the Harvard Kennedy School; 6% spontaneously suggested using $21 of aid to save time; 26 of 30 chose cash.
- Kenya (Whillans & West 2022, pre-registered, n = 1,070 working mothers in Kibera, Nairobi): three weeks of time-saving vouchers vs equal cash vs control; all three groups improved similarly; time-saving did not beat cash or control.
- Replication-style check (Lok & Dunn 2022, n = 15,545 Americans): buying time again linked to happiness, including for people living paycheck to paycheck, but very small (d = 0.10), about half the link for donating to charity (d = 0.21). Correlational.
- The Whillans 2017 SI appendix could not be retrieved; the video uses only main-text numbers.
