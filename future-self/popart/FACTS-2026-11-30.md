## 2. Loss-aversion results

### A. The plain-language result (Table 27.6, problems 1–4)

"The adjustments of mixed prospects to acceptability (problems 1–4) indicate that, for even chances to win and lose, a prospect will only be acceptable if the gain is at least twice as large as the loss. This observation is compatible with a value function that changes slope abruptly at zero, with a loss-aversion coefficient of about 2" (p. 688).

**Table 27.6, "A Test of Loss Aversion"** (p. 689). Values are median x. Each gamble is 50% lose c, 50% win x, compared with $0 for sure.

| Problem | 50% chance to lose | Median gain x needed | Ratio θ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $25 | **$61** | 2.44 |
| 2 | $50 | **$101** | 2.02 |
| 3 | $100 | **$202** | 2.02 |
| 4 | $150 | **$280** | 1.87 |

- Table note: "In each problem, subjects determined the value of x that makes the prospect ($a, ½; $b, ½) as attractive as ($c, ½; $x, ½). The median values of x are presented" (p. 689).
- Problems 5–6 compare one mixed gamble with another. For example: lose $20 / win $50 vs lose $50 / win x gives median x = $112, θ = 2.07. Lose $50 / win $150 vs lose $125 / win x gives x = $301, θ = 2.01.
- Authors' reading of problems 5–6: "when the possible loss is increased by k the compensating gain must be increased by about 2k" (p. 688).

### B. The lambda estimate

- **Estimated value:** "The median λ was 2.25, indicating pronounced loss aversion" (p. 689).
- **How it was estimated:** "we used a nonlinear regression procedure to estimate the parameters of equations (5) and (6), separately for each subject. The median exponent of the value function was 0.88 for both gains and losses" (p. 689).
- **Model behind it:** Equation (5) is a two-part power value function. It is v(x) = x^α for gains and v(x) = −λ(−x)^β for losses (p. 686).
- **Medians, not a mean:** 2.25 is the median of the 25 individual fits. "The parameters estimated from the median data were essentially the same" (p. 689).

### C. The paper's general statements

- "Loss aversion: One of the basic phenomena of choice under both risk and uncertainty is that losses loom larger than gains" (p. 674).
- "the principle of loss aversion according to which losses loom larger than corresponding gains" (p. 679).
- "The pronounced asymmetry of the value function, which we have labeled loss aversion, explains the extreme reluctance to accept mixed prospects" (p. 694).

## 3. Caveats in the paper itself

- **Small sample of students.** There were 25 Berkeley/Stanford graduate students (p. 682).
- **No real money on the line.** Choices were not paid out; there was only a $25 flat fee (pp. 682, 692). The authors argue this does not matter much: "all major violations of expected utility theory (e.g. the common consequence effect, the common ratio effect, source dependence, loss aversion, and preference reversals) were obtained both with and without monetary incentives" (p. 692). This argument relies on other studies, not on this one.
- **Parameter numbers are a convenience summary.** "For this reason, we focused here on the qualitative properties of the data rather than on parameter estimates and measures of fit. However, in order to obtain a parsimonious description of the present data, we used a nonlinear regression procedure..." (p. 689). The authors present 2.25 as a "parsimonious description", not a universal constant.
- **Noisy individual data.**
  - "the individual data, of course, reveal both noise and individual differences" (p. 684).
  - Session-to-session correlations "averaged .55 over six different prospects" (p. 684).
  - "Because individual choices are quite noisy, aggregation of problems is necessary" (p. 684).
- **The ratio varies with stake size.** θ ran from 2.44 for a $25 loss down to 1.87 for a $150 loss (Table 27.6, p. 689). "Twice" is approximate.
- **The theory is approximate.** "Theories of choice are at best approximate and incomplete" (p. 695).
- **The paper measures choices, not feelings.** Nothing in it measures how much a loss "hurts" or a gain "feels good". It infers asymmetric *value* from which gambles people accept.

## 4. Separate source: Brown, Imai, Vieider & Camerer (2024), JEL meta-analysis

I read the abstract, the introduction, the descriptive results (Table 4), the main Bayesian results (section 4.3), the publication-bias section and the opening of the Discussion. Pages are JEL journal pages.

- **Headline** (abstract, p. 485): "We examine 607 empirical estimates of loss aversion from 150 articles in economics, psychology, neuroscience, and several other disciplines. Our analysis indicates that the mean loss aversion coefficient is 1.955 with a 95 percent probability that the true value falls in the interval [1.820, 2.102]."
- **Same result in the results section** (p. 502, model M2): "The mean loss aversion parameter obtained from this estimation is 1.955, with a 95 percent CrI of [1.820, 2.102]."
- **Raw reported estimates** (pp. 488, 496): "the median value of the raw data points is 1.69 and the mean is 1.97." Also: "substantial mass (93.9 percent) on the range λ > 1".
- **After correcting for publication bias** (p. 508): "The 'corrected' mean λ is ... 1.743 with a 95 percent CrI of [1.672, 1.812]."
- **On the 1992 number** (p. 509): "Many other specifications are within 0.1–0.3 of this finding and produce credible intervals that do not include 1 or 2.25. The former number is consistent with no loss aversion; the latter is an early estimate from Tversky and Kahneman (1992) which seems a bit too high."
- **How they describe T&K 1992** (p. 486): "25 graduate students from elite west-coast American universities using three sessions of unincentivized lottery choice experiments. The median λ — no mean nor statistic of dispersion was reported — was λ = 2.25."

**What the Short can say.** "Roughly twice" is honest: T&K say "about 2" and the meta-analysis mean is 1.955. A more careful wording is "about 1.7 to 2 times", which covers the bias-corrected 1.74. **Do not say "2.25 times" as a general fact about people.** The meta-analysis says it "seems a bit too high".

## 5. Verdict on the proposed hook

**Proposed hook:** "Losing $100 hurts more than winning $100 feels good."

- **Direction: supported.** The paper states "losses loom larger than corresponding gains" (p. 679).
- **Not exactly what the paper measured, for two reasons:**
  1. The paper measures **choices about hypothetical gambles**, not pain or pleasure. "Hurts" and "feels good" are an interpretation, though a common one.
  2. The paper has **no $100-vs-$100 comparison**. The $100 data point is: to accept a 50-50 chance of losing $100, the median subject needed a win of **$202** (Table 27.6, problem 3).
- **Overall:** fine as colloquial framing, but it is not a fact from the paper. Better hooks use the paper's actual $100 → $202 number.
- **"Losses weigh roughly twice as much as gains":** acceptable. The paper says "about 2" (p. 688) and the meta-analysis says 1.955 (p. 485).
  - Do not say "exactly twice" or "2.25x for everyone".
  - Do not imply the 25 students bet real money.

## A. Lindqvist, Östling & Cesarini (2020): key facts with verbatim quotes

### Sample
- Country, timing, design (abstract, p. 2703): "We surveyed a large sample of Swedish lottery players about their psychological well-being 5–22 years after a major lottery event and analysed the data following pre-registered procedures."
- Size and prize pool (p. 2704): "because of the large sample size (3,362 players) and substantial prize pool ($277 million), our estimates have high precision relative to other work."
  - CAUTION: 3,362 is **players**, not winners. It includes 747 Kombi **non-winning matched controls** (Table 1, p. 2707). Table 4 (p. 2720) lists **Nwin = 2,589** winners for the Overall LS analysis, with mean prize 106K, max 1,557K and sum 275M (2011 USD).
- Three lotteries (p. 2705): "We elected to survey players from three of the four lotteries in the administrative sample: Kombi, Triss-Monthly, and Triss-Lumpsum."
- Prize sizes (p. 2706):
  - "Triss-Lumpsum winners (1994–2011) win a lump-sum prize between $7,000 and $700,000."
  - Triss-Monthly (1997–2011): "The size ($1,400–$7,000) and duration (10–50 years) of the supplement are determined by separate tickets."
  - Kombi "large-prize winners ... (defined as players who won at least 1M SEK)."
  - Kombi is "a monthly subscription lottery with approximately 500,000 subscribers, the proceeds of which are donated to the Swedish Social Democratic Party". Triss is "a highly popular scratch-off lottery run by the Swedish government-owned gaming operator, Svenska Spel".
- Which prizes drive the result (p. 2707): "most of our identifying variation comes from comparing non-winners and winners of small prizes with winners of prizes in the range $100,000–$800,000."
- Units (p. 2707): "lottery prizes are net of taxes and measured in units of year-2011 dollars."
- Survey year and response (pp. 2706–2707): survey mailed "In early fall of 2016" by Statistics Sweden. "To reduce experimenter demand effects, the letter made no mention of lotteries." Total response rate 69%.
- Years since win (p. 2709): "Because the lottery events in our sample took place between 1994 and 2011, the value of s varies between 5 and 22".

### Outcome measures (pp. 2708–2709). These are four different results.
- **Happiness**: "All things considered, how happy would you say that you are?" Scale 0–10.
- **Overall Life Satisfaction**: "Taking all things together in your life, how satisfied would you say that you are with your life these days?" Scale 0–10.
- **Mental Health**: GHQ-12, which asks how often specific feelings occurred over the last 2 weeks.
- **Financial Life Satisfaction**: rated on a 6-point scale.
- How they classify the measures (p. 2709): "Overall LS and Financial LS are evaluative ... our measure of Mental Health is affective". Happiness is described as "a hybrid of these two dimensions".

### Effect sizes (Table 3, p. 2712; text pp. 2704, 2711)
- p. 2704: "an after-tax prize of $100,000 improves life satisfaction by 0.037 standard deviation (SD) units. We find no evidence that the effect varies by years-since-win".
- p. 2704: "the estimated effects on our measures with a stronger affective component—happiness and an index of mental health—are smaller and not statistically distinguishable from zero."
- Table 3 (per $100K, in SD units):

| Outcome | Effect | p | Significant? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Happiness | 0.016 (SE 0.014) | 0.257 | **No** |
| Overall LS | 0.037 (SE 0.014) | 0.009 | Yes (FWER p 0.026) |
| Financial LS | 0.067 (SE 0.012) | <0.001 | Yes |
| Mental Health | 0.013 (SE 0.016) | 0.423 | **No** |

- Sample means (Table 3 note, p. 2712): Happiness 7.14/10 (SD 1.77); Overall LS 7.21/10 (SD 1.93).
- p. 2711: "Neither estimate is statistically distinguishable from zero, but for both outcomes, we can reject treatment effects equal to those found for Overall LS and Financial LS."

### How long it lasts
- p. 2715, among winners surveyed at least 11 years after the win: "the treatment-effect estimates range from 0.038 SD units (p = 0.062) for Happiness to 0.058 SD units (p = 0.004) for Overall LS."
- p. 2715: "there is little doubt that adaptation to the windfall is incomplete well over a decade after the lottery event."
- Caveat, p. 2723: "Since we did not survey any players within 5 years of the lottery, our research design is not suitable for studying short-run adaptation, but our results do reject the strong hypothesis of complete adaptation."

### Mechanism: satisfaction with money
- p. 2713: "including Financial LS as an additional control ... reduces the estimated effect of lottery wealth on Overall LS by 73%."
- p. 2713: winners do not blow the money. "lottery wealth dissipates slowly and is robustly detectable for well over a decade after the win".

### Benchmarks in plain words
- p. 2722 (good for viewers): "the long-run effects of $100K are comparable in magnitude to a reduction in daily commute time of 25–40 minutes (12.5–20 minutes for each one-way commute)."
- p. 2722: the short-run effects of major life events (unemployment, marriage, divorce, widowhood, birth of a child) are "large compared to the long-run effect of a $100K windfall".
- Scaling (p. 2715): "a $100,000 prize corresponds to an increase in net annual income of $5,996."

### Concluding sentence (p. 2724)
"Overall, our results suggest that wealth impacts people's satisfaction with their lives more than their affective well-being."

### What they say about Brickman 1978 (p. 2719)
> "The first study listed (Brickman et al., 1978) famously compared the happiness of 22 major lottery winners of the Illinois State Lottery to that of 22 controls domiciled in the same regions as the winners. The study found no statistically significant differences between winners and controls in terms of happiness (past, present, or expected future). After re-scaling, we obtained a treatment-effect estimate of 0.014 with a standard error of 0.025. This rescaled estimate is therefore quite similar to what we report for Happiness, both in magnitude (0.014 versus 0.016) and precision (0.025 versus 0.014). However, the prizes won by the 22 lottery players are very large ... with an average prize of $1.18M (range $123K to $2.46M)."

Those dollar figures are in 2011 dollars, not the nominal 1970s amounts. On p. 2722, Brickman "compared winners to controls recruited from the general population via phone books". Lindqvist do **not** call Brickman wrong or say it "fooled" anyone. Their harsh words about low power ("underpowered, perhaps dramatically so", p. 2719) are aimed at the later **mental-health** lottery studies (Lindahl; Gardner & Oswald; Apouey & Clark), not at Brickman.

## B. Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman (1978): key facts, from the full text

- Sample (p. 917, abstract): "Study 1 compared a sample of 22 major lottery winners with 22 controls and also with a group of 29 paralyzed accident victims".
- Prizes (p. 919): "Among the 22 lottery winners who were interviewed, 7 won $1 million, 6 won $400,000, 2 won $300,000, 4 won $100,000, and 3 won $50,000." These are nominal 1970s dollars.
- Selection (pp. 918–919): winners were picked "from a list of 197 major winners in the Illinois State Lottery". "In 20 of the 42 attempted interviews, the potential respondent was either unattainable or unwilling, resulting in a 52% response rate." Controls came from the phone book, with a 41% response rate.
- Timing (p. 920): "Only one of the winners had been aware of his good fortune for less than a month, and none of them had passed more than a year and a half with their new wealth." So they were interviewed **at most 18 months after winning**. The Swedish winners were surveyed 5–22 years after.
- Method (p. 919): most interviews were by phone (16 of 22 winners), done by student teams. Happiness was rated on a 0–5 scale: "how happy they were now (not at this moment, but at this stage of their life)".
- Results (Table 1, p. 921). Present happiness: winners **4.00**, controls **3.82**, accident victims **2.96**. Mundane pleasure: winners 3.33, controls 3.82.
- Text (p. 920): "Lottery winners and controls were not significantly different in their ratings of how happy they were now ... The F values for present, past, and future happiness were .27, 1.43, and .02". Winners rated ordinary activities as less pleasurable, F(1,42) = 7.05, p < .011.
  - The winners' average was slightly higher (4.00 vs 3.82), but the difference was not significant.
- Conclusion (p. 923): "Although lottery winners felt very good about winning the lottery, they took less pleasure than controls in a variety of ordinary events and were not in general happier than controls."
- The authors named their own limit (p. 924): "our data are sharply limited by the fact that they were obtained at a single point in time ... A larger study, preferably longitudinal, is needed".
- Winners rated winning 3.78 on a 0–5 best/worst scale. Accident victims rated their accident 1.28 (p. 920).
- Brickman measured **happiness and everyday pleasure only. It did not measure life satisfaction.**

## C. Verdict on the proposed hook

**"Lottery winners ARE happier. A tiny study fooled everyone." NOT accurate. Do not use it.**

1. **"ARE happier" overclaims.** In the Swedish data the **Happiness** effect is 0.016 SD per $100K, p = 0.257, **not significant**. Mental health is also not significant. What rose significantly and lasted was **life satisfaction** (0.037, p = 0.009) and **financial satisfaction** (0.067).
2. **"A tiny study fooled everyone" is not supported.** Lindqvist say Brickman's rescaled happiness estimate (0.014) is "quite similar" to their own (0.016). On happiness, the big study basically **agrees** with the small one. Brickman also flagged its own limits on p. 924. The "fooled everyone" idea comes from popular retellings, and neither paper documents it.
3. **The payoff line can be kept with fixes.** "The famous 1978 study had 22 winners" is true (Brickman p. 917; Lindqvist p. 2719). "Big Swedish lottery data found lasting life-satisfaction gains" is true (pp. 2703, 2715). Wording to watch:
   - Say "3,362 Swedish lottery players". Do not say "3,362 winners".
   - Say "life satisfaction", not "happiness".
   - Say "5 to 22 years later" or "still there over a decade later". Do not say "forever".

What the papers actually support: **Money didn't measurably raise winners' happiness, but it did raise how satisfied they are with their lives, mostly with their finances, and that lasted 5–22 years.** The 1978 study measured happiness within 18 months of the win and never asked about life satisfaction.

## D. Honest hooks (8 words or fewer, no "Why...")
1. **"Lottery winners aren't happier. They're more satisfied."** (7 words) Supported by Table 3, p. 2712.
2. **"The famous lottery study had just 22 winners."** (8 words) Supported by Brickman p. 917 and Lindqvist p. 2719. It works as a curiosity gap only if the payoff states the nuance.
3. **"Money didn't make them happier. It did this."** (8 words) The payoff is life satisfaction, mostly through satisfaction with finances.

## E. Mini-story beats (every number is sourced)
1. **Who and where.** Illinois, in a study published in 1978. Psychologists interviewed **22 lottery winners**; **7 had won $1 million** (Brickman p. 919). Each had won **no more than a year and a half** before (p. 920).
2. **Famous result.** The winners were "not in general happier" than 22 neighbors pulled from the phone book (p. 923). They even enjoyed everyday things like breakfast or TV *less* (pp. 919–920). Lindqvist call it the study that "famously" compared these groups (p. 2719). Avoid "everyone repeats" or "fooled everyone", because neither paper documents how widely it was cited.
3. **The authors' own warning.** "A larger study, preferably longitudinal, is needed" (p. 924).
4. **The bigger study.** Sweden. Researchers surveyed **3,362 lottery players** in 2016, **5 to 22 years** after their wins, with **$277 million** in prizes between them (Lindqvist pp. 2703–2704). The survey letter didn't mention lotteries (p. 2706).
5. **Twist, part 1.** On *happiness*, the big study agreed with the small one: no significant boost (0.016 vs Brickman's rescaled 0.014, p. 2719).
6. **Twist, part 2.** On *life satisfaction*, winners were clearly higher, and the gap was still there **more than a decade later** (pp. 2703, 2715). The main reason was satisfaction with their finances (p. 2713).
7. **Make the size concrete.** The long-run effect of **$100K** is about as big as cutting your daily commute by **25–40 minutes** (p. 2722).
8. **Moral.** Money may not change how you feel day to day. It does change how you judge your life (p. 2724).

- The brief describes "a 40-piece set with some broken pieces". That is correct, but the useful number is **31 intact pieces**. The abstract describes the set as "**31 intact pieces** (including the same 24) plus a few broken ones" (p. 107). 9 of its 40 pieces were broken.

## Dinnerware study (Study 4), pp. 116-117

### Design

  - The scenario was hypothetical. Nobody actually bought anything.

  - p. 113, fn. 2: "any responses over 3 standard deviations from the mean would be excluded. Only one response in Study 4 and none in any of the other studies was excluded under this criterion."

## Verdict on the hook "They added free plates. The set lost value."
**Not exactly true. Do not use it as written.**

1. **"Free" is invented.** No price was attached to the extra pieces. Each set's buyers stated a WTP for that set. Nothing was described as free or as a bonus.
2. **"Plates" is wrong.** The two sets had the same plates (8 dinner plates and 8 dessert plates each). The extra pieces were 8 cups and 8 saucers, and **9 of those 16 were broken**. The hook leaves out the broken pieces, and the broken pieces are what drove the effect. Without them there is no study.
3. **"Added" is fair only as a framing.** Set H "included all the pieces included in Set L, plus several more" (p. 116). But this was a between-subjects design. No one watched pieces being added to a set they had already valued. Different people saw different sets.
4. **"Lost value" means lower stated WTP.** It is not a market price or a resale value. The fair wording is "people would pay less", stated about a hypothetical clearance-sale purchase. It also holds only when the set is judged alone. Side by side, the bigger set won: $32.03 vs $29.70.

Accurate core claim: **A 40-piece set with 9 broken pieces (31 good) got a lower average bid ($23.25) than a 24-piece perfect set ($32.69), when people saw only one. Shown both together, they bid more for the bigger set.**

1. "More good dishes. Lower price. Here's how." (7 words)
2. "Nine broken cups and saucers cost this set." (8 words)
3. "24 perfect dishes beat 31 good ones." (7 words; true for separate evaluation only, so the story must show the side-by-side reversal)
- Backup: "Broken extras made people pay less." (6 words)

## Source and how it was read

## 1. Sample and procedure
- Dataset: "We used data from the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) Study of Early Child Care and Youth Development (SECCYD)" (p. 2).
- Sites: "Participants were recruited at birth from 10 U.S. sites across the country, providing a geographically diverse, although not nationally representative, sample" (p. 3).
- Size: children with "a valid measure of delay of gratification at age 54 months, as well as nonmissing achievement and behavioral data at age 15 (n = 918)" (p. 3).
- Focal subsample: "children whose mothers did not report having completed college when the child was 1 month old (n = 552, a sample that is 10 times larger than the sample size in the Shoda et al., 1990, study)" (p. 3). Children of college-graduate mothers: n = 366 (Table 1, p. 3).
- Age at test: 54 months. The abstract says "age 4" (p. 1). Test year: "the 54-month interview (i.e., preschool; 1995–1996)" (Table 1 note, p. 3).
- Treat: "An interviewer would present children with an appealing edible treat based on the child's own stated preferences (e.g., marshmallows, M&M's, animal crackers)" (p. 4).
- Rules: "If the child waited for 7 min, the interviewer would return, and the child could eat the treat and receive an additional portion as a reward for waiting. Children who chose not to wait could ring a bell ... The measure ... was ... the number of seconds the child waited, with 7 min being the ceiling" (p. 4). The paper says "an additional portion". It does NOT say "double" or "two marshmallows".
- Versus Mischel: "the 7-min cap was much shorter than Mischel's maximum assessment length; the children in Mischel's sample were asked to wait between 15 and 20 min" (p. 4).
- Ceiling: "approximately 55% of children hit the 7-min ceiling" (p. 4). "Children whose mothers obtained college degrees hit the ceiling at a rate of 68%, compared with 45% for children whose mothers did not complete college (p < .001)" (p. 4).
- Condition: children got "only the version of the task that Shoda and colleagues (1990) called the diagnostic condition (i.e., the children were not offered strategies and were able to see the treat as they waited)" (p. 5).
- Outcome: achievement at Grade 1 and age 15, using the Woodcock-Johnson (WJ-R) math (Applied Problems) and reading tests, averaged into a composite (p. 5). Behaviour: mother-reported internalizing and externalizing problems on the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) (p. 5).

## 2. How it differs from the original Mischel/Shoda studies
- Original kids: "children who attended a preschool on the Stanford University campus" (p. 1).
- Sample sizes: "based on very small and highly selective samples of children from the Stanford University community (ns = 35–89 ...)" (p. 2).
- Follow-up: "Although Mischel's original work included over 600 preschool-age children ... Shoda and colleagues were able to contact only 185 of the original 653 children" (p. 2).
- Original correlations: "For the 35 to 48 children who were tested in the diagnostic condition ... Shoda and colleagues (1990) observed large correlations between delay time and SAT scores, r(35) = .57 for math, r(35) = .42 for verbal, and ... parent-reported behaviors ... r(48) = .39. These bivariate correlations were not adjusted for potential confounding factors" (p. 2).
- Who is in the new sample: in the non-college-mother group, about 16% were Black, 14% of mothers had not finished high school, and "between 17% and 18% of families were living at or below the poverty line" (p. 4). Hispanic children "were still underrepresented" (p. 4).
- The authors call this a "conceptual, rather than traditional, replication" (p. 2).

## 3. Raw link: waiting vs. achievement (children of non-college mothers, n = 552)
- Abstract: "an additional minute waited at age 4 predicted a gain of approximately one tenth of a standard deviation in achievement at age 15. But this bivariate correlation was only half the size of those reported in the original studies and was reduced by two thirds in the presence of controls for family background, early cognitive ability, and the home environment" (p. 1).
- Grade 1: "the bivariate association between minutes waited and academic achievement was 0.28 (SE = 0.04, p < .001), considerably less than the .57 correlation Shoda and colleagues found for SAT math scores and the .42 correlation they found for verbal scores" (p. 9).
- Age 15 (the headline number): "β = 0.24, SE = 0.04, p < .001" (p. 9). Table 4 col. 4 gives 0.236 (p. 10).
- Wording caution: the abstract ties "one tenth of an SD per minute" to age 15. The results section ties it to Grade 1 (p. 9). Don't put this number on screen. If you must use it, say "about one tenth of a standard deviation" and nothing more precise.

## 4. How much it shrank with controls (age 15, non-college mothers)
- Background + home controls: "fell substantially when controls for earlier characteristics were added, β = 0.08, SE = 0.03, p = .016" (p. 9). Going from 0.24 to 0.08 is the "reduced by two thirds" in the abstract (p. 1).
- Adding age-54-month skills: "became nonsignificant when 54-months controls were added, β = 0.05, SE = 0.03, p = .140" (p. 9).
- Discussion: "models with controls for concurrent measures of cognitive skills and behavior reduced the association between delay of gratification and age-15 achievement to nearly zero" (p. 17).
- What the controls were. This matters for the "money" claim.
  - Set 1, "Child background and HOME controls" (pp. 5, 7): gender, race, birth weight, mother's age at birth, mother's education, family income ("average of all nonmissing income data" from 1-54 months, log-transformed), mother's vocabulary test score (PPVT, called "a commonly used measure of intelligence"), and early child cognition. The early cognition measures are the Bayley MDI at 24 months and the Bracken school-readiness test at 36 months. Set 1 also includes temperament at 6 months, nine HOME home-environment subscales at 36 months, and site dummies.
  - Set 2, "Concurrent 54-month controls" (p. 7): WJ-R Letter-Word, Applied Problems (early math), Picture Vocabulary, Memory for Sentences and Incomplete Words, plus CBCL internalizing and externalizing at 54 months.
  - So the "two thirds" drop already includes early cognitive ability at 24-36 months, together with income and the other family variables. The paper never isolates income or "money" on its own.
- Who waited the full 7 minutes (non-college mothers, Table 3, p. 6): log family income 0.89 vs 0.57 for those who didn't wait (p < .001). Mother's PPVT 96.43 vs 90.47. Bayley MDI 93.89 vs 85.91. Proportion Black .07 vs .24. Text: "those who completed the delay-of-gratification task were from higher income families (p < .001) than noncompleters, had mothers with higher PPVT scores (p < .001), and had higher scores on dimensions of the HOME observational assessment (ps = .04 to < .001)" (p. 8).

## 5. The 20-second threshold
- "Most of the variation in adolescent achievement came from being able to wait at least 20 s" (abstract, p. 1).
- "most of the achievement boost for early delay ability was gained by waiting a mere 20 s" (p. 14).
- Raw age-15 gaps vs. kids who waited under 20 s (Table 4, p. 10): 0.353 SD for 20 s-2 min, 0.457 for 2-7 min, 0.646 for the full 7 min. Text: "nearly three fourth of a standard deviation for the group that waited the entire 7 min" (p. 9). With background controls, all three groups fall to "between 0.23 and 0.30" and are "not close to being statistically significantly different from one another (p = .752)" (p. 9).
- In the lower-SES sample, "23% waited less than 20 s". In the higher-SES sample, "only 10% of children waited less than 20 s" (p. 8).

## 6. Behaviour outcomes
- "we were surprised to find virtually no relationship—even in the absence of controls—between delay of gratification and the composite score of mother-reported internalizing and externalizing at either Grade 1 or age 15" (p. 9).
- Abstract: "Associations between delay time and measures of behavioral outcomes at age 15 were much smaller and rarely statistically significant" (p. 1).
- Alternative measures (risky behaviour, impulse control, youth self-reports): "we still found virtually no associations" (p. 14).

## 7. Results by mother's education
- Mean wait: 3.99 min (SD 3.08) for non-college mothers vs 5.38 min (SD 2.62) for college-graduate mothers (Table 2, p. 4; text p. 8).
- Children of college-graduate mothers (n = 366): raw age-15 β = 0.17 (p = .007). The link "became statistically indistinguishable from zero once controls were added". The 7-min group estimate was "surprisingly small and negative (β = −0.04, SE = 0.15, p = .816)" (p. 11). The authors warn "it is difficult to draw strong conclusions from these models given the imprecise nature of their coefficient estimates" (p. 11).
- SES interaction: "None of the interactions tested were statistically significant ... it [is] impossible to provide a definitive answer to whether the relation between early delay ability and later achievement differs by SES" (p. 14). So the paper does NOT show that the test works differently for rich and poor kids.

## 8. What the test seems to measure (self-control vs. cognition)
- "the marshmallow test had the strongest correlation with the Applied Problems subtest of the WJ-R, r(916) = .37 ... correlations with measures of attention, impulsivity, and self-control were lower in magnitude (rs = .22–.30)" (p. 14).
- "the marshmallow test should not be thought of as a mere behavioral proxy for self-control, as the measure clearly relates strongly to basic measures of cognitive capacity" (p. 14).
- "controlling for a composite measure of self-control explained only about 25% of our reported effects on achievement. These results suggest that the marshmallow test may capture something rather distinct from self-control" (p. 17).
- Attention and impulse measures (CPT) "accounted for only 21% to 27% of the effect" (p. 14).
- Interventions: "an intervention that altered a child's ability to delay but failed to change more general cognitive and behavioral capacities would likely have limited effects on later outcomes" (p. 17).

## 9. Authors' own caveats
- Not causal: "we do not claim that the associations we estimated reflect causal impacts" (p. 7). Also: "variation in our delay-of-gratification measure at age 54 months was not exogenous" (p. 17).
- Leftover bias: "If remaining unobserved factors also contribute ... bias in our estimates may still remain" (p. 8).
- Ceiling: "The 7-min ceiling was limiting". For the higher-SES group it "prevented a direct replication" (p. 17).
- Representativeness: "data not drawn to be nationally representative provide a shaky foundation for generalization" (p. 17).
- "The design and analysis plans for the study were not preregistered" (p. 18).
- Bottom line: "although early delay of gratification did indeed correlate with later achievement for children whose mothers had not completed college, the magnitude of this association was highly sensitive to the inclusion of control variables and did not appear to be linear" (p. 17).

## VERDICT on the proposed hook and payoff
- "The marshmallow test may have measured money, not willpower" is NOT faithful to the paper. Don't use it.
  - The paper never isolates money. Income is one control among many: race, mother's education, mother's vocabulary score, the home environment, early cognitive tests, temperament and birth weight. Together these cut the link by two thirds.
  - The strongest correlate the authors highlight is early MATH skill (r = .37), not income. Their own reading is that the test "may capture something rather distinct from self-control" and relates "strongly to basic measures of cognitive capacity". That makes the test partly about thinking skills and partly about family background.
  - "Not willpower" overstates. Self-control measures explained "only about 25%", which is some, not zero.
  - The SES interaction was inconclusive (p. 14). Don't claim the test "works for rich kids only".
- The planned payoff is broadly accurate if worded carefully.
  - OK: "bigger and more diverse sample". Use 918 kids total and 552 in the main analysis, against 35-89 kids in the Stanford follow-ups.
  - OK: "half the size of the original" (abstract wording).
  - OK: "two thirds smaller after accounting for family background, the home, and early thinking skills".
  - OK: "near zero once you also account for skills at age 4". The paper says "nearly zero", β = 0.05, not significant.
  - Fix: credit the two-thirds drop to "family background, home environment AND early cognitive ability", not to family background alone.
  - Fix: say "age 4" (paper's abstract) or "four-and-a-half" (54 months). Don't use "two marshmallows" or "double". The paper says "an additional portion".
- A safe reworded version of the original hook: "The marshmallow test measured more than willpower." (7 words)

## HOOKS (8 words or fewer, myth-bust, no "Why")
1. "The marshmallow test wasn't measuring what you think." (8 words). Supported by p. 14 and p. 17: "may capture something rather distinct from self-control".
2. "Twenty seconds mattered more than seven minutes." (7 words). Supported by p. 1 and p. 14: "most of the achievement boost ... gained by waiting a mere 20 s".
3. "Retest the marshmallow test: two-thirds of it vanishes." (8 words). Supported by p. 1: "reduced by two thirds" once background, home and early cognition are controlled.

## The paper's explanation (p. 457)

Definitions given earlier on pp. 456-457:
> "we use the notion of a psychological account, defined as an outcome frame which specifies (i) the set of elementary outcomes that are evaluated jointly and the manner in which they are combined and (ii) a reference outcome that is considered neutral or normal." (p. 456)
> "We propose that people generally evaluate acts in terms of a minimal account, which includes only the direct consequences of the act." (p. 456)
> "People commonly adopt minimal accounts because this mode of framing (i) simplifies evaluation and reduces cognitive strain, (ii) reflects the intuition that consequences should be causally linked to acts, and (iii) matches the properties of hedonic experience..." (p. 457)

## 1. Original paper: Shu, Mazar, Gino, Ariely & Bazerman (2012)

Source: PMC3458378, read via WebFetch (see caveat above). Page numbers are not available from that route.

**Field study (Experiment 3, auto insurance)**
- Setting (summary from WebFetch): an automobile insurer in the southeastern United States. Customers were randomly given the original form (sign at the end) or a new form (sign at the beginning). Data Colada confirms: "a field experiment (N = 13,488) conducted by an auto insurance company in the southeastern United States".
- Statement signed: "both of which required their signature following the statement: 'I promise that the information I am providing is true.'"
- Sample: "We compared the reported current odometer mileage on 13,488 completed policy forms for 20,741 cars"
- Size of the gap: "The difference was 2,427.8 miles per car."
- Percent effect: "Asking customers to sign at the beginning of the form led to a 10.25% increase in the calculated miles driven"
- Means (from WebFetch; matches Kristal 2020 Table 1): sign at top 26,098.4 miles (SD 12,253.4); sign at bottom 23,670.6 miles (SD 12,621.4); F(1, 13,485) = 128.63, P < 0.001.
- Money claim: "suggesting a minimum average difference of $48 in annual insurance premium per car between customers in the two conditions."
- Caveat the authors gave, quoted by Data Colada fn 3: "miles driven per car have been accumulated over varying unknown time periods" (p. 15200).
- Why higher miles counts as "more honest": reporting more miles means a higher premium, so the paper treated higher reported mileage as more honest reporting. (This is the paper's logic, given in the WebFetch summary, not a verbatim quote.)

**Lab studies** (from WebFetch, not checked line by line)
- Exp 1, n = 101, three conditions. Share who cheated: 37% sign at top, 79% sign at bottom, 64% no signature. χ²(2, n = 101) = 12.58, P = 0.002. Verbatim: "fewer cheated in the signature-at-the-top condition (37%) than in the signature-at-the-bottom and no-signature conditions".
- Exp 2, n = 60 (30 per condition). Cheated: 37% (11/30) top vs 63% (19/30) bottom. Ethics-related word completions: 1.40 signed first vs 0.87 signed last (this was the paper's "ethics salient" measure).
- Kristal 2020 independently confirms: "two laboratory experiments ( n = 101 and n = 60, respectively) and one field experiment ( n = 13,488)".

## 2. The replication: Kristal, Whillans, Bazerman, Gino, Shu, Mazar & Ariely (2020)

Source: PMC7132248 (CC BY), read in full. PNAS 117(13):7103-7107, first published 16 March 2020.

- Who ran it: five of its seven authors also wrote the 2012 paper. "In 2012, five of the current authors published a paper in PNAS showing that people are more honest when they are asked to sign a veracity statement at the beginning..." (Significance statement)
- Design and n: "Across five conceptual replications ( n = 4,559) and one highly powered, preregistered, direct replication ( n = 1,235) conducted with the authors of the original paper, we observed no effect of signing first on honest reporting." (Abstract)
- Total: "After conducting six studies that were larger and more highly powered ( n = 5,794), including one direct replication of one of the two original PNAS laboratory studies, we did not replicate a sign-first effect." (Discussion)
- Direct replication (Study 6): "A sample of 1,235 adults was recruited across four laboratory sites" (Harvard, Boston University, and two University of Chicago Booth labs). It was "a direct replication of study 1 from Shu et al." with the no-signature control left out. It "had the power to detect an effect size of d = 0.10 at 80% power."
- Result: "We failed to detect an effect of signing first on all three preregistered outcomes" (d = 0.07, 0.04, 0.06, with every 95% CI including zero; corrected values from the 2026 correction). "This laboratory experiment provides the strongest evidence to date that signing first does not encourage honest reporting."
- Effect sizes compared (Table 2): original lab Study 1 d = −1.05 vs replication Study 6 d = 0.04 [−0.07, 0.15].
- Bottom line in the authors' words: "we can conclude from these data that there is no good evidence for the existence of this effect, and the limited times it has been documented in the literature are likely a type 1 error." Also: "we recommend that practitioners take this finding out of their intervention 'tool-kit' as it is unlikely to increase honesty."
- Real-world reach: "Since this finding was published, various government agencies have adopted this practice." (Abstract) "The original finding has been cited 345 times in peer-reviewed publications".
- What it did NOT do: it did **not** re-run the insurance field study. It replicated lab Study 1 and ran five related studies.
- What the authors found in the original field data: "the reported odometer readings in the two conditions were significantly different at baseline." "One possible interpretation of the baseline difference in reported miles driven is that the randomization failed (or may have even failed to occur as instructed) in that study." Baseline (before the study): sign-at-bottom mean 75,034.50 miles vs sign-at-top mean 59,692.71, t(13,474) = 17.78, P < 0.0001 (Table 1; Discussion). They also wrote that they "are concerned with its potential flaws, including the significantly different odometer readings at baseline." They posted the original field data at osf.io/3javq.
- Side detail: "When one of the authors (Whillans) worked with a local government, they spent $15,000 of labor costs and 6 mo trying to implement this seemingly simple change." (footnote: "This effort was before the replications were conducted and failed for other administrative reasons.")
- **2026 correction** (PNAS 123(27): e2610441123, 29 Jun 2026): fixes Table 2 and Study 4–6 details. Study 4 had 912 participants across three conditions, 743 in the two analyzed. In Study 6 the d values should be positive and one t value is 1.25. The editor's note says the authors first contacted the journal in 2021 and the correction was not published then "due to an oversight". **None of this changes the headline finding of no effect.**

## 3. Data Colada [98] (Simonsohn, Simmons & Nelson, with anonymous researchers), 17 Aug 2021

Source: datacolada.org/98, read in full. All of these are **Data Colada's findings and conclusions**, attributed to them.

- Origin: "This post is co-authored with a team of researchers who have chosen to remain anonymous. They uncovered most of the evidence reported in this post."
- How it was found: the 2020 authors "posted the data of their replication attempts and the data from the original 2012 paper". The anonymous team downloaded it and found "There is very strong evidence that the data were fabricated."
- Their summary of the original result: "Customers assigned to the 'sign-at-the-top' condition reported driving 2,400 more miles (10.3%) than those assigned to the 'sign-at-the-bottom' condition."
- Baseline gap: "The condition difference before random assignment (~15,000 miles) was much larger than the analyzed difference after random assignment (~2,400 miles)".
- **Anomaly #1, uniform miles driven:** miles driven for car 1 is "visually and statistically ( p =.84) indistinguishable from a uniform distribution ranging from 0 miles to 50,000 miles". "There are 1,313 customers who drove 40,000-45,000 miles, 1,339 customers who drove 45,000-50,000 miles, and zero customers who drove more than 50,000 miles." "The highest value in the dataset is 49,997, and it appears only once." The same pattern holds for all four cars (all ps > .78). Their proposed mechanism: a random number "capped at 50,000 miles" was added, "easy to do in Excel (e.g., using RANDBETWEEN(0,50000))."
- **Anomaly #2, no rounding at Time 2:** "multiples of 1,000 and 100 were disproportionately common in the Time 1 data, they weren't more common than other numbers in the Time 2 data." People rounded in the earlier readings, but nobody rounded in the experiment's readings.
- **Two fonts:** "half of the data in that column are printed in Calibri, and half are printed in Cambria." For car 1 baseline: "Calibri font for 6,744 customers ... and Cambria font for 6,744 customers".
- **Anomaly #3, near-duplicates:** "There are 22 four-car Calibri customers in the dataset ... All of them have a Cambria driving twin: a Cambria-fonted customer whose mileage for all four cars is greater than theirs by less than 1,000 miles." "We ran 1 million simulations ... it didn't happen once." "These data are not just excessively similar. They are impossibly similar."
- **Anomaly #4:** Calibri baseline mileages are rounded, but the Cambria ones are not.
- Conclusion: "the data underwent at least two forms of fabrication: (1) many Time 1 data points were duplicated and then slightly altered (using a random number generator) to create additional observations, and (2) all of the Time 2 data were created using a random number generator that capped miles driven, the key dependent variable, at 50,000 miles." "We know, beyond any shadow of a doubt, that they were [fabricated]."
- **On who did it, Data Colada itself says:** "it is impossible to tell from the data who fabricated it." They list three possibilities and do not choose one. **The Short must not name anyone or repeat footnote 14 (file metadata).**
- All original and 2020 authors were contacted. Four of the 2012 authors posted responses, which I did not read.

## 4. PNAS retraction notice

Shu, Nina Mazar, Francesca Gino, Dan Ariely, and Max H. Bazerman, which was first published August 27, 2012; 10.1073/pnas.1209746109 (Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. U.S.A. 109, 15197–15200).
> The editors are retracting this article and note that Simonsohn, Simmons, and Nelson (http://datacolada.org/98) have provided evidence to question the validity of the data in the article.

- **Who requested it:** the notice says "**The editors** are retracting this article." It does not say the authors requested it. (News reports say the authors asked for it. I did not verify that, so leave it out.)
- **Stated reason:** evidence "to question the validity of the data". The notice does **not** use the words "fabricated", "fraud" or "misconduct". I checked: "requested", "fabricat" and "fraud" do not appear on the page.
- The whole paper was retracted, lab studies included. The notice does not say which study.

## 5. What is NOT established by these documents (keep out of the Short)
- Who fabricated the data. Data Colada says it is "impossible to tell from the data." Name no one and do not hint.
- Any finding by a university or official misconduct inquiry. None of these sources reports one.
- That the journal found fabrication. The journal said only that the validity of the data was questioned.
- That the authors requested the retraction. The notice says the editors retracted it.
- That the replication re-tested the insurance study. It did not. It replicated lab Study 1 and ran five related studies.
- That signing (or honesty pledges) never works. Kristal et al. limit their claim to top vs bottom placement and say they make no claim about "signing versus not signing at all".
- Order of events: replication failed in **March 2020**, fabrication evidence came in **August 2021**, retraction in **September 2021**. The replication did not come after the retraction.

## 6. Verdict on the proposed hook and payoff

- **Hook, "The famous honesty study had a secret."** Partly fair, but risky. "Secret" suggests someone knowingly hid something, which no official document says. It also gives nothing concrete. Better to say what was hidden in the data (fonts, 50,000-mile cap) and not imply who hid it.
- **Payoff part 1, "retracted after its field data were found to be fabricated."** Not exactly true as one statement. The retraction notice says only that Data Colada "provided evidence to question the validity of the data." The word "fabricated" is Data Colada's conclusion. Safe wording: "Independent researchers found evidence the insurance data were fabricated. The journal retracted the paper." Or: "...concluded the data were fabricated..." with the conclusion credited to the researchers.
- **Payoff part 2, "a large replication found signing first did nothing."** Basically true but needs tightening. Better: "A replication with 1,235 people, run with the original authors, found no effect of signing first," or "six studies, 5,794 people, no effect." "Did nothing" is slightly strong. The authors say "no good evidence for the existence of this effect." The replication was of a lab study, not the insurance study, and it came out in 2020, before the fraud finding.
