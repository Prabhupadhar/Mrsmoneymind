## Fact check (read against the papers on 20 Nov 2026)
- **s06**: Dunn, Aknin & Norton (2008), *Science* 319: 1687-1688, bonus study. 16 employees, profit-sharing bonus
  averaging $4,918.64; happiness 6-8 weeks later predicted by the share spent prosocially (gifts, charity), not by
  bonus size. Voice says "about five thousand dollars" and flags the small sample.
- **s07**: Whillans et al. (2017), *PNAS* 114: 8523-8527, study 9 (n = 98 working adults, hypothetical $40 windfall):
  "Only 2.0% spontaneously reported that they would make a time-saving purchase." Study 8 shows time-saving
  purchases gave greater end-of-day positive affect than material ones.
- **s08**: same paper, study 6 (n = 818 Dutch millionaires). The text says "just under half ... spent no money
  outsourcing disliked tasks", but Table 1 lists 60.3% as buying time (about 40% not). Because the two figures
  differ, the backlog hook "Half these millionaires..." was softened to "a big share", which both support.
  Buying time was linked to greater life satisfaction across the paper's samples.
- **s09**: same paper, study 8: working adults in Vancouver (n = 60), $40 on two consecutive weekends, one
  time-saving and one material purchase (order counterbalanced). Time-saving purchase: lower time stress,
  higher positive affect, lower negative affect at end of day.
- **s10**: Knutson, Rick, Wimmer, Prelec & Loewenstein (2007), *Neuron* 53: 147-156. 26 subjects analysed (others
  excluded for motion or too few purchases); real purchases (one trial per session counted). "Excessive prices
  activated the insula"; right insula activation during the price period predicted decisions not to purchase.
  The authors link the insula to anticipated pain and loss, so the voice says "tied to pain, and to expecting a
  loss" (not disgust). "Price alarm" is a metaphor; the closing line uses "might".
