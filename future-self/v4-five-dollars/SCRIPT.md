# The $5 Experiment (v1 script)

About 540 spoken words. Expect roughly 2:40 of voice, based on video 3's pace. Voice only (Kokoro), and each line has a **mood** (curious, shocked, firm, excited, sad, whisper, warm or neutral).
The narrator talks to the viewer. The screen never reads the line back. Pop words are in **bold**.
The look and characters are the same as videos 1 to 3: the kid in the mustard hoodie, navy and orange scenes, handwritten pop words. The neighbor (cap, green jacket) from video 3 comes back, this time as the person the kid buys a coffee for.
New: **a toddler** (a smaller, rounder kid with a bib) and **Monkey**, a sock puppet.

- **Short 2, "Toddlers already know this"**: part three (the toddler and Monkey).

## Fact sources
- Dunn, E. W., Aknin, L. B. & Norton, M. I. (2008). Spending money on others promotes happiness. *Science* 319(5870): 1687–1688.
  - Experiment: N = 46. Morning happiness rating, an envelope with $5 or $20, spend by 5:00 p.m. that day. Personal: a bill, an expense or a gift for themselves. Prosocial: a gift for someone else or a charitable donation. Phoned after 5:00 p.m. Prosocial spenders were happier; the amount had no effect.
  - Prediction survey: N = 109 students at the same university. 69 vs 40 thought personal spending would make them happier; 94 vs 15 thought $20 would beat $5. The paper calls them "doubly wrong."
  - National survey: 632 Americans. Personal spending was unrelated to happiness; prosocial spending predicted greater happiness. People spent more than 10 times as much on personal as on prosocial spending.
  - Bonus study: 16 employees, profit-sharing bonus averaging $4,918.64. Happiness 6–8 weeks later was predicted by the share spent on others, not by the bonus size.
  - Authors at the University of British Columbia (Vancouver) and Harvard Business School. "Vancouver … university campus" in the script rests on the UBC affiliation and "students at the same university."
- Aknin, L. B., Hamlin, J. K. & Dunn, E. W. (2012). Giving leads to happiness in young children. *PLoS ONE* 7(6): e39211. 20 toddlers (about 22–23 months). Happier giving treats (Goldfish crackers or Teddy Grahams) to a puppet, "Monkey", than receiving treats; happier giving their own treat than a "found" one.
- Aknin, L. B., Dunn, E. W., Proulx, J., Lok, I. & Norton, M. I. (2020). Does spending money on others promote happiness?: A registered replication report. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 119: e15–e26. Students who bought for a stranger in need were happier; recalling past spending showed no effect. The authors conclude the effect exists but its size depends on the method.
- Kim et al. (2022). Prosocial spending encourages happiness: A replication of the only experiment reported in Dunn, Aknin, and Norton (2008). *PLoS ONE* 17: e0272434. N = 133. The original analysis found no significant difference; an exploratory one-item measure favored prosocial spending.
