# Fact checks for the HyperFrames Shorts (each paper read in full before writing)

## h01-traders: Barber & Odean 2000, "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth", J. Finance 55(2)
- 66,465 households with common stocks, large US discount broker, Jan 1991 - Dec 1996 (78,000 sampled).
- Households sorted into fifths by monthly turnover. Top fifth: average monthly turnover 21.49% (>250%/yr).
- Net annualized geometric mean return: most-active fifth 11.4%, least-active fifth 18.5%; value-weighted market 17.9%.
- Gross returns barely differ across fifths (18.5-18.7%): the gap is trading costs.
- Average household turns over more than 75% of its portfolio a year.
- Safe hook: "The busiest traders earned the least." (net returns). Do not say "lost money": 11.4% is still a gain.

## h02-default: Madrian & Shea, "The Power of Suggestion" (NBER WP 7682, 2000; published QJE 2001). Read in full (WP version).
- One large US Fortune 500 company (health care and insurance). From 1 Apr 1998 new hires were automatically enrolled
  in the 401(k) unless they opted out; nothing else about the plan's money changed (same match, same funds).
- At 3-15 months of tenure: 37% of the cohort hired just before (WINDOW) participated vs 86% of those hired under
  automatic enrollment (NEW). "Less than half."
- Default: 3% contribution, 100% in the money market fund. 76% of NEW participants contributed exactly 3%
  (about 10% in older cohorts). 61% of the NEW cohort sat fully at the default; only 1% of earlier cohorts chose it.
- Those who opted out did so in the first month (15%).
- Safe hook: "Same job. Same plan. One switch: 37% to 86%." Twist: most stayed at the default 3% in cash-like fund.

## h03-locker: Milkman, Minson & Volpp 2014, "Holding the Hunger Games Hostage at the Gym", Management Science. Read in full (author manuscript).
- 226 students, faculty and staff at a large Northeastern US university, fall 2011; randomized into 3 groups (75/75/76).
- Full treatment: an iPod with 4 tempting audio novels of their choice, kept in a monitored locker at the gym: listen only while exercising.
  Most popular picks: The Hunger Games trilogy.
- First week after intake: full treatment +0.48 visits/week vs 0.94 in control = 51% more. The effect faded over time,
  especially after Thanksgiving break.
- End of study: offered a one-month program that would hold THEIR OWN new iPod at the gym. 64% said it sounded appealing;
  61% (100% - 39.3% who said $0) stated a willingness to pay above $0; average $6.91.
- Safe hook: "They paid to have their own iPod locked away." (they stated a willingness to pay, elicited with an incentive-compatible method).

## h04-match: Choi, Laibson & Madrian, "$100 Bills on the Sidewalk" (NBER WP 11554, 2005; REStat 2011). Read in full (WP).
- Year-end 1998 data, 7 US firms (Hewitt Associates). Sample: 5,045 match-eligible employees over 59½ who could
  withdraw 401(k) money for any reason with no penalty, so contributing below the match threshold loses free money.
- Between 20% and 60% (median 31%) did not get the full match (Table 3, conservative loss definition).
- Average loss among those losing: $160 (0.66% of pay) to $782 (2.32% of pay) a year; largest single loss $7,596 (6% of pay).
- 2004 field experiment at Company A: treatment survey explained the match, the free withdrawals and had them compute
  their loss (median answer $1,200 a year). Contribution rates two months later: +0.16% of pay (treatment) vs +0.08%
  (control); difference 0.08% of pay, not significant. "Information did not meaningfully raise contributions."
- Safe hook: "Free money. No catch. They left it."

## h05-reminder: Karlan, McConnell, Mullainathan & Zinman, "Getting to the Top of Mind: How Reminders Increase Saving"
(Yale EGC discussion paper 988, 2010; Management Science 2016). Read in full (discussion paper).
- Field experiments with 3 banks (First Valley Bank, Philippines; Caja de Ica, Peru; Ecofuturo, Bolivia) with people who
  had just opened goal-oriented savings accounts. Monthly reminders randomly assigned: text messages (Philippines,
  Bolivia) or letters (Peru), vs no reminder.
- Reminders: about 6% more saved (p 0.065-0.079) and 3 percentage points more likely to reach the savings goal.
- Peru: reminders that mentioned the client's own specific goal raised savings by about 16% vs no reminder; reminders
  without the goal had no effect. A photo of the goal or a jigsaw-puzzle piece of it did not significantly affect savings.
- Safe hook: "A text a month. They saved more." (do not say "one text").

## h06-farmers: Duflo, Kremer & Robinson, "Nudging Farmers to Use Fertilizer" (NBER WP 15131, 2009; AER 2011). Read in full (WP).
- Busia district, Western Kenya; parents of grade 5-6 pupils at 16 schools; programme run with ICS in 2003-2004.
- Half a teaspoon of top-dressing fertilizer per plant: about 36% return over a season (70% annualized).
- 97.7% of farmers in the demonstration-plot programme said they planned to use fertilizer next season; only 36.8% did.
- SAFI: a field officer visited right after harvest and offered fertilizer at the regular price with free delivery.
  Season 1: 45% of offered farmers used fertilizer vs 34% of the comparison group (+11 points; 46-63% increase).
- Free delivery offered later (top-dressing time): 20% bought vs 39% under SAFI at harvest; use rose 9-10 points,
  not significant, less than half SAFI's effect. A 50% subsidy late in the season: +13-14 points, similar to early free delivery.
- Safe hook: "98% planned it. 37% did it." (rounded 97.7 / 36.8).

## h07-quiz: Lusardi & Mitchell, "The Economic Importance of Financial Literacy: Theory and Evidence" (NBER WP 18952, 2013;
J. Economic Literature 2014). Read in full (WP).
- The "Big Three" questions: $100 at 2% for 5 years (more than $102 / exactly / less); 1% interest vs 2% inflation after
  1 year (buy more / same / less); "Buying a single company stock usually provides a safer return than a stock mutual fund"
  (true/false). Correct: more than $102; less; false.
- 2004 HRS (age 50+): only about half answered the interest and inflation questions; only a third got all three.
- Across countries, "in the U.S., only 30 percent can" answer all three correctly.
- 2009 US Financial Capability Study: 70% rated themselves 4 or higher out of 7, but only 30% of the same people could
  answer the factual questions.
- Risk diversification is the hardest: 34% of US respondents said "do not know".
- Safe hook: "Three money questions. Only 30% pass."

## h08-peers: Beshears, Choi, Laibson, Madrian & Milkman, "The Effect of Providing Peer Information on Retirement Savings
Decisions" (NBER WP 17345; J. Finance 2015). Read in full (WP).
- Large US manufacturing firm, about 15,000 US employees; mailing on 30 July 2008 (natural field experiment).
- Non-participants got a Quick Enrollment form: tick a box to start saving 6% of pay (default fund). Randomly, some letters
  added "Join the A% of B-C year old employees at [company] who are already enrolled" (A ranged 77%-93%).
- Among non-participants with a 0% default (not auto-enrolled, mostly union workers), peer information reduced enrollment
  from 9.9% to 6.3%, about a third lower; a higher reported peer % lowered enrollment further.
- The backfire was concentrated among employees with low relative income: discouragement from upward comparison.
- Safe hook: "Told 'everyone saves'? They saved less." (true for that group; do not generalize to all workers).

## h09-75k: Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers 2023, "Income and emotional well-being: A conflict resolved", PNAS 120(10)
e2208661120 (open access, PMC10013834). Read in full.
- Kahneman & Deaton 2010: more than 450,000 Gallup responses (2008-09); emotional well-being flattened beyond about
  $75,000 (midpoint of the $60-90K category; "at or below 90K").
- Killingsworth 2021: 1,725,994 experience-sampling reports from 33,391 employed US adults ("How do you feel right now?"
  on their phones); average happiness rose with log(income) well beyond $200,000.
- Adversarial collaboration reanalysis: flattening exists, but only for the least happy 15-20%; it levels off at $100,000
  (inflation-adjusted threshold). For the happier majority, happiness keeps rising; for the happiest ~30% it rises even faster
  above $100k.
- The effect is small: correlation 0.09; median happiness at $15,000 vs $250,000 differs by about 5 points on a 100-point scale.
- Safe hook: "$75k is enough? The data disagrees."

## h10-lockbox: Dupas & Robinson, "Why Don't the Poor Save More? Evidence from Health Savings Experiments"
(NBER WP 17255, 2011; AER 2013). Read in full (WP).
- Rural Kenya; 113 ROSCAs (savings groups) randomized to control or 4 savings devices; 771 individuals in 6- and 12-month follow-ups.
- Safe Box: a locked metal box with a deposit slit, a padlock, the key given to the participant, plus a passbook naming the
  health goal. Lockbox: identical, but the program officer kept the key.
- Over 12 months, the Safe Box increased preventative health investment by about 68% vs control; the Lockbox had no effect
  on investment over the year (liquidity cost outweighed benefits). Health Pot (ROSCA side pot): about +129%.
- Mechanism: mental accounting; once money was set aside, people resisted unplanned spending.
- Take-up: 71% of Safe Box group still had money in it at 12 months.
- Safe hook: "A metal box. Health spending up 68%." (preventative health investment).

## h11-age53: Agarwal, Driscoll, Gabaix & Laibson, "The Age of Reason: Financial Decisions over the Lifecycle"
(NBER WP 13191, 2007; Brookings Papers 2009). Read in full (WP).
- Ten credit markets (home equity loans and lines, auto loans, card APRs, mortgages, small-business cards, late/over-limit/
  cash-advance fees, balance-transfer use), proprietary US lender data; controls for credit risk (FICO etc.).
- Younger and older adults paid higher interest rates and more fees than middle-aged adults; costs minimized around age 53
  (mean "age of reason" 53.3); a peak in the 40-60 range cannot be rejected for all ten.
- Home equity lines: 75-year-olds pay about $265 more per year than 50-year-olds; 25-year-olds about $295 more.
- Balance-transfer "Eureka" task (14,798 borrowers): about a third never found the optimal strategy.
- Leading explanation: experience rises with age while analytic function falls (2-3% of a standard deviation per year).
- Safe hook: "Your money brain peaks at 53." (financial-decision costs lowest around 53 in these data).

## h12-payday: Agarwal, Skiba & Tobacman, "Payday Loans and Credit Cards: New Liquidity and Credit Scoring Puzzles?"
(NBER WP 14659, 2009; AER Papers & Proceedings 2009). Read in full.
- Individual-level match of a large payday lender's loans with a major card issuer's accounts: 3,090 people with both.
- Payday loans: typical first loan about $300, due next payday; annualized interest rates of at least several hundred percent.
- Two-thirds of the matched sample had at least $1,000 of credit-card liquidity on the day of their first payday loan.
- Typical card holders would have saved almost $200 (over the year after the first payday loan) by using card liquidity.
- Card liquidity had fallen by $545 on average over the year before the first payday loan.
- Taking a payday loan predicts a 92% higher serious card delinquency rate in the next year.
- Safe hook: "Payday loan. $1,000 free on their card." (two-thirds of the matched sample).

## h13-gas: Hastings & Shapiro, "Mental Accounting and Consumer Choice: Evidence from Commodity Price Shocks"
(NBER WP 18248, 2012; QJE 2013). Read in full (WP, key sections).
- Panel: over 10.5 million gasoline transactions from 61,494 households at a large grocery retailer with on-site gas
  stations, 2006-2009 (plus EIA aggregate data 1990-2009).
- A $1 increase in the price of gasoline raises a typical household's propensity to buy regular by 1.4 percentage points.
  The average household buys about 1,200 gallons a year, so that equals a $1,200 income loss; but cross-sectionally a
  $1,200 income reduction raises the propensity to buy regular by less than 0.1 percentage point.
- Second half of 2008: gasoline prices fell during the financial crisis and recession; although consumer spending and
  well-being indicators were plummeting, households substituted to higher-octane gasoline.
- Placebo: gas prices had no significant effect on quality choice for milk or orange juice brands.
- Safe hook: "Gas got cheaper. They bought fancier gas."

## h14-gym: DellaVigna & Malmendier, "Overestimating Self-Control: Evidence from the Health Club Industry"
(NBER WP 10819, 2004; AER 2006 "Paying Not to Go to the Gym"). Read (abstract, introduction and stylized facts in full).
- Three US health clubs; 7,978 members over three years.
- Members on a flat monthly fee of over $70 attended on average 4.8 times per month (first six months): more than $17
  per expected visit, although a $10-per-visit fee was available. Only 20% of monthly users paid less than $10 per visit.
- Survey: members overestimate future usage by more than 100%.
- Average gap of 2.29 full months between last attendance and cancelling a monthly contract, costing $185.
- The average non-subsidized monthly user forgoes savings of about $700 per membership (of about $1,500 paid).
- Safe hook: "$17 a visit. A $10 pass existed."

## h15-tax: Chetty, Looney & Kroft, "Salience and Taxation: Theory and Evidence" (NBER WP 13330, 2007; AER 2009).
Read (abstract, introduction and experiment design in full).
- Field experiment with a major grocery chain, one supermarket, three weeks in early 2006. Shelf prices excluded a 7.375%
  sales tax (added at the register). For 750 taxable products (cosmetics, hair care accessories, deodorants), tags showing
  the tax-inclusive price were posted below the regular price tag.
- Quantity sold and revenue of treated products fell by about 8% relative to control products and nearby control stores,
  close to the effect of a 7.375% price increase.
- Survey: the median shopper correctly reported the tax status of 7 of 8 products and the average sales tax rate within
  0.5 points: they know, but don't compute tax-inclusive prices.
- Alcohol: state excise taxes (in the posted price) cut consumption about an order of magnitude more than sales taxes.
- Safe hook: "Same price. Different tag. 8% fewer sales."

## h16-salary: Card, Mas, Moretti & Saez, "Inequality at Work: The Effect of Peer Salaries on Job Satisfaction" (NBER WP 16396, 2010; AER 2012).
Read (abstract, introduction, design, data and main results in full).
- Early 2008: after a court decision on California's "right to know" law, the Sacramento Bee launched a website to search the
  salary of any state employee, including University of California staff and faculty.
- Oct 2008 to May 2009: a random subset of employees at UC Santa Cruz, UC San Diego and UCLA got an email telling them the site
  existed; 3-10 days later all employees were emailed a survey.
- Use of the site: 19.2% in the control group; the email raised it by about 28 points to about 48%. Most new users (80%)
  looked up colleagues in their own department.
- Main analysis sample: 6,411 respondents matched to salary data (85% staff, 15% faculty).
- Below the median of their unit and occupation: lower pay and job satisfaction and a higher stated likelihood of looking for a
  new job (t about 2-3). Above the median: no significant change ("report no higher satisfaction").
- Caveats: ~20% survey response rate; no detectable effect on actual turnover 2-3 years later. Script says "more likely to job
  hunt" (stated intention), not "quit".
- "Half got sadder": below-median earners are by definition about half of each unit; the effect is an average for that group.
- Safe hook: "They saw their coworkers' pay. Half got sadder."

## h17-coin: Levitt, "Heads or Tails: The Impact of a Coin Toss on Major Life Decisions and Subsequent Happiness" (NBER WP 22487, 2016; Review of Economic Studies 2021).
Read (abstract, introduction, design, results, biases and conclusion).
- FreakonomicsExperiments.com launched 23 Jan 2013, ran about a year. People stuck on a decision picked a question; one choice was
  heads, the other tails (mostly heads = make the change). About 23,500 tosses, 22,511 usable. Follow-up surveys at 2 and 6 months.
- Most popular "important" question: "Should I quit my job?" (2,186 tosses).
- Heads made people 24.9 percentage points more likely to report making a change at two months (all questions; about one-third as
  large for important questions, still highly significant).
- Important decisions (quitting a job, breaking up, ...): those who changed were happier at six months, and the 2SLS (coin as
  instrument) estimate is large and significant at six months; job quitting and breaking up individually large, positive, significant
  at six months. Less important decisions: no measurable effect.
- Author: "people may be excessively cautious when facing life-changing choices"; results "merely suggestive".
- Caveats (in description): self-selected online sample of people on the fence, self-reported happiness, survey non-response.
- Safe hook: "A coin said quit. Six months later: happier." (the coin is the instrument; "they" = people the coin pushed to change).

## h18-fafsa: Bettinger, Long, Oreopoulos & Sanbonmatsu, "The Role of Simplification and Information in College Decisions: Results from the H&R Block FAFSA Experiment" (NBER WP 15361, 2009; QJE 2012).
Read (abstract, introduction, design and main results).
- 2008, H&R Block offices in most of Ohio and the Charlotte, North Carolina area; low- and moderate-income families.
- The 2008 FAFSA: eight pages, over 100 questions (plus worksheets).
- FAFSA treatment: software pre-filled the form from the tax return; the tax professional asked the remaining questions, "which
  took less than 10 minutes"; plus a personalised aid estimate.
- Dependent students (HS seniors / recent grads): FAFSA filing 40.2% control, +15.7 points with help. College enrollment the next
  fall: 26.8% (control) to 34.5% (FAFSA treatment), about +29% relative.
- Information-only group (personalised aid estimate, no help with the form): no significant effect on FAFSA filing; small sample
  for dependents, but the help and info-only effects differ at 5%. Script: "applications didn't budge" (filing).
- "The form was the wall" is our interpretation, in line with the authors' simplification conclusion.
- Safe hook: "10 minutes of help. More kids in college."

## h19-teacher: Fryer, Levitt, List & Sadoff, "Enhancing the Efficacy of Teacher Incentives through Loss Aversion: A Field Experiment" (NBER WP 18237, 2012).
Read (abstract, introduction, program details, implementation and main results).
- 2010-11 school year, Chicago Heights, IL (30 miles south of Chicago): nine K-8 schools, ~3,200 students; 150 teachers (93.75% of
  eligible) opted in and were randomised.
- Reward: $80 per percentile, max $8,000; expected value $4,000 (~8% of average salary).
- "Gain": paid at the end of the year. "Loss": $4,000 at the start of the year, return the difference if performance was below average,
  up to $4,000 more if above. Identical net pay for a given performance; only timing and framing differ.
- Math (ThinkLink): Loss +0.220 sd, 6.84 percentile points (significant); Gain +0.092 sd, 1.88 points (insignificant). Loss and Gain
  differ at 95%+. State tests (no incentives attached): Loss +0.201 sd.
- "About seven percentile points" = 6.84. "Barely moved" = small, insignificant gain effect.
- "Losing money hurts more than missing it" = loss aversion, the paper's mechanism.
- Safe hook: "$4,000 up front. Their students scored higher."

## h20-photo: Bertrand, Karlan, Mullainathan, Shafir & Zinman, "What's Psychology Worth? A Field Experiment in the Consumer Credit Market" (NBER WP 11892, 2005; QJE 2010 as "What's Advertising Content Worth?").
Read (abstract, introduction, market, design and results sections).
- A large South African consumer lender (150+ branches; experiment in 86 branches) mailed 53,194 former clients loan offers at randomly
  assigned interest rates from 3.25% to 11.75% per month (standard rates 7.75%-11.75%). Two mailings (late Sep, late Oct); no year
  in the WP text, so the Short gives none.
- Independently randomised "features": comparisons, number of example loans, suggested uses, a photo of a smiling person in the
  corner (none, or varied by race and gender), a promotional lottery.
- Price: a 1-point rate cut raised take-up about 0.26 points (~3.5%).
- Photo: for male customers, a female photo instead of a male photo significantly raised take-up, "about as much as dropping the
  interest rate 4.5 percentage points" (monthly rate). No significant pattern for female customers; authors cannot say whether the
  female photo helps or the male photo hurts. Script: "swapping a man's photo for a woman's" = exactly that comparison.
- Also: one example loan instead of many: +9% demand (= 2.3-point monthly rate cut).
- Safe hook: "A woman's photo. Men borrowed like rates dropped."

## h21-cycle: Shapiro, "Is There a Daily Discount Rate? Evidence from the Food Stamp Nutrition Cycle" (working paper Aug 2003; J. Public Economics 2005).
Read (abstract, introduction, data, monthly patterns, hypothetical-choice and food-insufficiency evidence).
- Data: USDA Continuing Survey of Food Intake by Individuals 1989-91 (nationally representative; 24-hour recall + 2-day diaries) and
  the Maryland EBT survey 1992-93 (1,298 food stamp households).
- Caloric intake of food stamp recipients declines 10-15% over the 30 days after benefit receipt (0.40-0.45% per day).
- Benefit dates: "virtually no states vary the benefit date systematically by month" (same day each month).
- Maryland: "Would you take less than $50 today rather than $50 in a month?" In week four, seven percent more households say yes than in
  week one (+0.35 points per day). Script: "more families" (no number on screen beyond the hook's calories figure).
- Author's explanation: short-run impatience (time preference), rejecting food depreciation, strategic sharing and confusion.
  Script line "Week one just feels more urgent" paraphrases that.
- Caveats: cross-sectional; old (1989-93) US data. "Split the money by week" is our suggestion, not the paper's.
- Safe hook: "Money day: full plates. Day 30: up to −15% (calories)."

## h22-door: DellaVigna, List & Malmendier, "Testing for Altruism and Social Pressure in Charitable Giving" (NBER WP 15629, 2009; QJE 2012).
Read (abstract, introduction with the four main results, design).
- Door-to-door fund-raising for a local children's hospital and an out-of-state charity; 7,668 households in towns around Chicago,
  April-October 2008.
- Treatments: baseline (no warning); Flyer on the doorknob the day before with the one-hour visit window; Opt-out flyer with a
  "Do Not Disturb" box.
- Opening the door: 41% baseline; 10% lower with the flyer, 25% lower with the opt-out flyer ("a quarter fewer").
- Giving: 6.3% of households in baseline and Flyer; opt-out flyer cut giving 30%, driven by donations up to $10.
- Mail/Internet: only one contacted household out of 7,668 gave that way.
- Structural estimate: social pressure cost of saying no is $3.5 (in-state charity), $1.4 (out-of-state). Script: "about three
  dollars fifty" for the local charity case (screen labels it local charity).
- Safe hook: "One do-not-disturb box. Donations: −30%."

## h23-bonus: Ariely, Gneezy, Loewenstein & Mazar, "Large Stakes and Big Mistakes" (Federal Reserve Bank of Boston WP 05-11, July 2005; Review of Economic Studies 2009).
Read (abstract, introduction, Experiment 1 India and Experiment 2 MIT).
- Experiment 1: 87 residents of a rural town in India (Madurai area), late 2002; six games (memory, creativity, motor skills);
  randomly assigned to small, moderate or very large incentives: full payment Rs 4, 40 or 400 per game for "very good", half for "good".
- Rs 400 "relatively close to" the rural all-India average monthly per capita consumer expenditure (Rs 495) -> "close to a month's
  spending per person".
- Share of games at "very good": low 25.6%, mid 22.2%, high 6.3%. Earnings share: low 35.4%, mid 36.7%, high 19.5%. Low vs mid n.s.;
  high lower than both.
- Experiment 2 (MIT, 24 students, within-subject): high incentives (10x) significantly improved key-pressing (pure effort) and
  marginally reduced adding performance (p = 0.056). Script: "hurt adding up numbers" (marginal; FACTS notes it).
- Abstract: "With some important exceptions ... high reward levels can have detrimental effects on performance." The MIT key-pressing
  line is the exception shown in the Short.
- Safe hook: "Biggest bonus. Worst results." (true across the three India groups)
